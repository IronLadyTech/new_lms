What is already known about a deadline evening, at no cost. The way a database of this kind fails under a rush is specific and well documented: it tolerates enormous numbers of writes spread across many records, but only about one write per second to any single record. Systems fall over on deadline night when every submission also updates something shared — a running total, a progress counter, a batch summary — because that shared record becomes a queue everybody waits in.

This product has no such record. Every write it makes is filed under one learner, or under one learner and one task. A thousand people submitting at the same moment are writing to a thousand different records, which is the arrangement the database is built for. That was established by reading the code rather than by running anything, so it holds regardless of what equipment is available.

It does not prove a deadline evening will be smooth — only measurement does that, and measurement needs the practice environment. It does mean the one fault that would make it certain to struggle is absent, which is worth knowing before deciding how urgently to fund the environment.

The gap worth closing next is writing at scale — and that handoff is to the business. Engineering has the harness, the staging project id, and the safety rails that refuse production. What is left is linking a billed Firebase account so those tests can run against a real cloud database without touching learners.

What the system enforces

Below are the rules the LMS applies on its own, without anyone having to remember them. They are business decisions — who may see what, what unlocks when, what counts as finished — written into the product. Every rule here is covered by automated tests, so a future change that breaks one is caught before it reaches a learner.

Rule What the system does Why it matters
Programme order LEP → 100 Board Members → MBW. A learner sees all three; only the ones they are entitled to will open. Locked programmes stay visible, so the path ahead is clear rather than hidden.
Payment decides access Unpaid opens nothing. Registration deposit opens the early sections. Full payment opens the programme. Read from Zoho, which spells the same status several ways. Anything unrecognised is treated as unpaid — it errs toward locking, never toward giving content away.
Sections unlock in sequence A section opens only when the one before it is finished, the deposit or full payment is in, and the learner is in a batch — whichever that section requires. A paying learner still cannot skip to Quarter 3, and a learner who has finished Quarter 1 still cannot continue on a deposit alone.
Every submission is reviewed Work goes Locked → Available → Submitted → Under review → Complete. A reviewer chooses approve, ask for improvement, or reject. Work sent back does not count as done, does not add to progress, and stays the learner's next task. Work already submitted does not block them from carrying on.
Five levels of access Guest → Learner → CX → Admin → Super Admin, each including everything below it. CX can review and manage their batches but cannot change roles or delete a cohort. Admins cannot delete accounts. Only Super Admin can.
CX sees only their own batches A CX member sees learners in batches assigned to them, within their own programme. One cohort's staff cannot see another cohort's learners.
Streaks count consecutive days Any activity counts the day. A second activity the same day adds nothing. A missed day resets to one. Days are measured in India time wherever the learner or server is, so someone working at half past midnight gets credit for the right day.
Attendance A learner may mark themselves present but never absent. Only staff can record an absence. Below 60% flags the learner as at risk. Attendance records cannot be quietly improved by the person being measured.
Guest browsing A guest session lives in the browser and disappears when the tab closes. Guests cannot read course content, submit anything, or be enrolled. Lets a prospective learner look around without creating an account.

One rule worth a decision from you

Needs a business call

"Rejected" is final. Of the three review outcomes, approve and ask-for- improvement both leave the learner a way forward. Reject does not: there is no resubmission, no appeal, and no route back for that piece of work.

That may be exactly right. But it is the one rule where the system makes a decision a human might later want to reverse, and it is worth confirming that is intended rather than inherited. If it is not, allowing a reviewer to reopen a rejected submission is a small change.

The layout finding — found by measurement, now fixed

This was the largest remaining gap when the assessment began. It is included because how it was found matters as much as the fix: the numbers below came from measuring the running product at five screen sizes, not from an opinion about how it looked.

The desktop was the phone layout, stretched

Closed

On a 1,440px screen, learner pages rendered a single column 1,328px wide. Body text therefore ran to roughly 200 characters per line against a readable maximum of about 75, and a support field spanned 1,278px to hold a one-line subject. The admin user list was single-column with no grid at any width, so staff scrolled through records that would have fitted on one screen.

The cause was not missing design work. The layout offered four different widths depending on the page, but a more specific rule elsewhere overrode all of them with "no limit" — and that rule applied to every learner page. The intended widths had never once taken effect, and the shell quietly changed width as you moved between pages.

Now. One width system: pages grow with the screen up to 1,280px and then stop, so a large monitor gets comfortable margins instead of stretched content. Above 1,024px, settings and support pages lay out in columns, dashboard panels sit side by side, and admin lists become real rows. Nothing below 1,024px changed — the phone layout was already right.

How it was caught. The new responsive check failed on its first run at 1440px and 1920px. Without it, the fix would have been applied, produced no visible change, and looked like it had worked.

Performance test

The last open reliability question was what the product does with a large intake. It had never been tested, so the honest answer was "nobody knows". It has now been measured against a throwaway copy of the database filled to production scale. No live data was touched.

In one paragraph

The database was loaded with 1,000 learners and 8,000 pieces of submitted work, and every screen was opened five times and timed. Learners are unaffected by growth entirely — their screens take about a second whether the programme has 100 people or 1,000. Staff screens were the problem, and three of the four have been fixed; they now sit at three seconds where two of them previously took over a minute. One screen still fails: the analytics page, which can take anything from 20 seconds to 100. It is a reporting screen rather than something used hour to hour, and it is the last item on the list.

Pass / fail criteria

Thresholds are Nielsen's response-time limits, not preference. One second is the limit for a user's train of thought to remain unbroken; ten seconds is the limit for holding their attention at all. A staff screen doing real work sits between the two.

Result Slowest run Meaning
Pass ≤ 3s The screen keeps up with the person using it.
Concern 3–10s Usable, but the wait is noticed. Acceptable occasionally, not all day.
Fail > 10s Attention is lost; staff will assume it is broken and reload.

Judged on the slowest of the five runs rather than the average, because the worst run is what a user actually meets and then remembers.

Staff tools were the scaling problem (now mostly fixed). Every screen lands around a second at both measured sizes, and two are quicker with the larger cohort. That last part is the giveaway: if growth were costing anything, no screen could be faster with ten times the learners. The differences are the ordinary variation of any measurement, not a trend.

The proof is not really in the seconds, though — it is in what gets sent. Opening the MBW page transfers 1,094 bytes whether the programme has 100 learners or 1,000. Not "about the same": the identical number, to the byte, because the page asks for that learner's own record and nothing else. Adding learners cannot slow down a request that was never looking at them.

Those seconds are the database's share, not the learner's experience. They were measured with the site already downloaded and no network delay, which isolates the question this section is about — does the cohort size matter — and answers it cleanly. It is not what a learner feels on a first visit. Measured again cold, on a phone-grade 4G connection with a slower processor, the same dashboard took 8.3s and pulled 930 KB. It now takes 4.4s and pulls 604 KB.

That is a separate problem with a separate cause, and it is not about scale — it is the same on day one with ten learners. Roughly a third of that weight is avoidable:

What is downloaded Now Why it is more than it needs to be
Three programme cover images 277 KB 1024×576 photographs, sent at full size to fill a small card. Also saved as .png while actually being JPEGs.
Logo 57 KB 2125×2133 pixels, displayed at 48. Around forty times more image than the page ever shows, on every screen.

The images are done. Resizing them to the size they are actually displayed at removed 233 KB and took the first visit from 8.3 seconds to 4.4, with nothing on screen changing — every picture is still larger than the space it occupies, and the lettering on the programme covers stays sharp. The logo alone had been forty-four times wider than the space it is shown in.

What is left of that list is the fonts: four weights of the body typeface load where the design uses two, which is roughly 50 KB more. That one is a design decision rather than a technical one, so it is left open.

One thing to watch as time passes: the dashboard reads every announcement ever published. With three years of them the page grows from 615 elements to 3,640. It is not slow today, but it is the same unbounded pattern that made the review queue unusable, and it should be capped before it becomes one.

Still untested: a large number of learners submitting work at the same moment — a deadline evening. Reading scales flatly, as shown; writing has not been put under the same test.

CX-LOAD-01 — the review queue put every submission on the page at once

Closed

The reviews screen rendered the entire queue with no limit. At 1,000 learners that was 8,000 rows and just over 50,000 elements on a single page — enough to make the browser itself struggle, on top of the wait for the data.

Now. The queue shows 50 submissions and a Show 50 more control. Nothing is hidden — every item is still reachable, and the counts, filters and totals are still calculated over the whole queue, so no number on the screen changed. Only the amount drawn at once is bounded.

Measured effect at 500 learners: the page went from 22,134 elements to 487 — about 45× less for the browser to hold — and it stays flat as the cohort grows instead of climbing.

CX-LOAD-02 — two screens were loading data they never showed

Closed

The batches screen downloaded every submission in the programme and then displayed none of them — it only lists batches and their learners. The batch-analysis screen downloaded every submission in the programme and then discarded all but the one batch being looked at.

Measured effect at 1,000 learners: the batches screen went from tens of seconds to 1.3s, and the data it pulls fell from about 80 KB to 2.8 KB. Each screen now asks for what it actually shows.

CX-LOAD-03 — the review queue now reads a page at a time

Closed

The reviews screen no longer downloads the programme to work out what to show. It asks the database for three totals and one page of fifty submissions, then fetches only the learners named on that page. Show more fetches the next fifty.

Measured at 1,000 learners: 71.3s → 4.1s, and the data pulled fell from about 80 KB to 10 KB. Crucially the figures on screen are unchanged — the queue total, "needs review" and "awaiting resubmit" were checked against the old behaviour and came back identical (8,000 / 4,000 / 2,000), because the totals are now counted by the database over the whole queue rather than by the browser over everything it downloaded.

The database indexes this needs are live, and were confirmed against the real project before the change was merged rather than assumed.

CX-LOAD-04 — the home page now counts instead of fetching

Closed

The home page downloaded every learner and every submission to produce three numbers and eight rows. At 1,000 MBW learners that was 69.6s. It now fetches learners only — a thousand load in about a second, and the participant count has to stay exact — and asks the database for the rest: the backlog is a count, the preview is eight rows, and the completed-work figure is one count per active task. 69.6s → 5.9s.

This had been flagged as needing a business decision, on the grounds that the completion percentage could not be counted without shifting it. It could. Both versions were run against the same database and every figure came back identical — 1,000 participants, 6,000 needing attention, 16% complete. No decision was required after all.

OPS-02 — the analytics screen

Open

One screen remains, and it is the only outright failure in the results above: 57s on average, 101s at worst, against a ten-second limit. Its best run was 19.5s, so it is unpredictable as well as slow.

What it costs. This is how progress across batches is seen, so at that speed it gets avoided rather than used — decisions get made without it. Nothing else on the product is affected: the daily CX screens are between 3.1s and 3.6s at 1,000 learners, and the learner site is untouched throughout.

What it needs. 1–2 days. The groundwork is already built and live; what remains is the last step, and the design is settled rather than open.

Why this one took longer than the others

The screens already fixed needed totals, which a database answers directly. These charts do not ask how many tasks are complete — they ask how each learner is doing. A learner counts as needing attention if any one task was sent back, and as finished only if every task is done. That is a judgement about a person across all 29 of their tasks, and no total produces it.

So the answer now lives on the learner's own record, kept current as they submit — the same pattern the product already uses for streaks and last-active dates. That is built, tested and live, along with a script to fill it in for learners who already have work. None of it changes anything on screen yet.

The final step was built and withdrawn: the screen drew before its fallback data arrived, so the completion figure read 0% for about fifty seconds before correcting itself. A wrong number that quietly fixes itself is worse than a slow correct one. It was caught by running the old and new versions against the same database and comparing every figure, and re-applying it means making each figure read as unavailable until its data is there.

On several staff at once — what can and cannot be claimed. Eight staff were put on the review queue together at 1,000 learners, before and after the fix. Before, five of the eight never loaded at all; after, all eight succeed. That comparison is sound, because both runs used the same equipment.

The timings from those runs are not sound, and are therefore not quoted. Screen time rose from 2 seconds with one browser to 8 with two, 40 with four and about 100 with eight — roughly five times worse per doubling. Nothing in the product behaves that way; that is one laptop running eight browsers and the database at once, and what it measures is the laptop. Answering the concurrency question properly needs load generated from separate machines, which is worth doing before a large intake and has not been done.

Findings by area

Video & task completion

Verified end to end

A learner can open a lesson, watch the video, submit work, receive feedback and resubmit. This is now proven against a real database rather than by inspection: 12 tests drive the actual submit, review and resubmit code.

Three defects here were fixed during this work. Watch progress was held only in memory, so a learner who watched 85% of a 40-minute video and lost signal returned to zero — it now saves every 15 seconds and on leaving the page. Videos autoplayed muted, which reads as broken audio. And the gate claimed to track YouTube watch time, which it cannot; the wording now matches what the system actually does.

Backend & data validation

Strong

The database rules are what actually protect learner data — the interface can be bypassed from any browser console. 53 tests now verify them, written from the attacker's side: a learner cannot promote themselves to admin, unblock themselves, grant themselves paid access, read another learner's submissions or tickets, or open a locked resource.

These tests were themselves verified. Deliberately weakening one rule failed exactly the three tests guarding it, and no others — proof the suite would catch a real mistake rather than passing regardless.

One access-control flaw was found and fixed: a function deciding which learners a staff member may see reported every unassigned learner as a batch member if the batch had not finished loading. Not reachable in the product, but it failed open rather than closed — the wrong default for anything governing who sees whose data.

Error handling

Good

20 screens carry a failure state with a retry, 14 show a loading skeleton rather than a frozen blank, and 10 use a proper empty state that explains what will appear there. Going offline is signalled in all three workspaces.

Two silent failures were fixed. The learner dashboard used to show a confident, empty dashboard when its data failed to load — indistinguishable from a genuinely empty account. The course page could show a loading skeleton forever.

Performance & reliability

Good

First load is 61% lighter than at the start of this work: previously every visitor downloaded the entire admin system just to reach the login form, roughly 14 seconds on a mid-range Android phone. Lesson pages shed a further 162 kB.

Database costs were growing with the size of the database rather than with usage — the admin panel read every historical record on each page load and discarded all but the newest hundred. Now bounded and paginated.

Verified by network traffic, not by reading the code. Loading an MBW lesson fetches 59 files; the 162 kB video-streaming library is not among them. It is downloaded only when a lesson actually uses self-hosted streaming video, which is the intended behaviour.

Admin & CX workflows

Adequate — throughput-limited

Every operational task is reachable and works: managing participants, batches, attendance, course access, announcements, tickets, and the review queue. Destructive actions ask for confirmation. Admin sections are now individually linkable, so a colleague can be sent straight to the right screen.

The gap is throughput, not capability. The review queue has no multi-select and no bulk approve, so clearing 47 submissions is 47 separate open–read–decide–save cycles, around 90 minutes of uninterrupted clicking. Adding bulk actions and keyboard navigation would cut that to a handful of passes.

A dashboard defect here was material: the "needs attention" figure was structurally incapable of exceeding 8, so staff saw 8 while 47 learners waited. Fixed.

User flow

Adequate

No journey dead-ends, and no step is broken. Two are longer than they need to be.

Returning learner. Fixed. Resume was the fourth of nine sections, behind a banner and two summary blocks — below the fold on a phone. It now sits directly under the greeting, at 251px on a phone against a fold at 812px, with reference material collapsed beneath.

Finding a specific lesson. Fixed. Search existed but was a small secondary button, so in a 100-task programme learners scrolled instead. It is now presented as a search field, which is what makes people realise search is there at all.

Prospective learner. Guest mode locks every screen and offers no route to buy. An enquiry form now captures the lead properly, but there is still no self-service path from interest to enrolment.

Severity here means consequence, not effort: Critical — work or data can be lost. High — someone cannot do their job. Medium — a real cost, worked around today. Low — noticeable, not obstructive. No Critical or High defects remain open other than the analytics screen.
